<div class="quip">Wisconsin is a one-party consent state, which means you can record the officer while they explain why cheese curds do not count as a valid excuse for speeding. They do not, but the recording might keep you out of jail.</div>

<div class="settled">
<b>The stuff that is actually true in Wisconsin</b>
<ul>
<li><strong>You must show your license, registration, and proof of insurance.</strong> Wis. Stat. 343.18, 341.05, and 344.01. All three. The officer can demand them on the spot. Failure to display is a fine of $10-$200. But if you produce valid license in court, charge may be dismissed.</li>
<li><strong>You can record the officer.</strong> Wisconsin is a one-party consent state (Wis. Stat. 968.31). You are legally permitted to record your own interactions with police. Courts have consistently held that recording police in public is protected by the First Amendment. Just do not interfere.</li>
<li><strong>They cannot make you wait for a drug dog.</strong> <em>Rodriguez v. United States</em> (2015). Once the ticket is written, the stop is over. Wisconsin courts follow this.</li>
<li><strong>You can refuse to consent to a search.</strong> Fourth Amendment. Say "I do not consent to a search." If they have probable cause (smell, plain view), they can search anyway. But your refusal matters in court.</li>
<li><strong>Passengers do not have to show ID.</strong> Wisconsin has no stop-and-identify statute for mere passengers. But if the officer has reasonable suspicion that the passenger committed a crime, that changes.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="varies">
<b>The grey area where people get themselves arrested</b>
<ul>
<li><strong>"I forgot my license at home."</strong> Wis. Stat. 343.18 is a fine offense. You can be ticketed. But if you produce a valid license in court, the charge may be dismissed.</li>
<li><strong>"I do not answer questions."</strong> True for most questions, but 343.18 requires you to display your license. You cannot refuse that one.</li>
<li><strong>"You cannot search my car."</strong> Correct, but Wisconsin has a broad plain-view doctrine. If the officer sees contraband, they do not need consent. Also, the smell of marijuana alone is not probable cause in Wisconsin after legalization, but the officer may claim other factors.</li>
<li><strong>"I am driving to a safer location."</strong> No Wisconsin statute gives you this right. The officer may interpret it as evading. Use your judgment, but know the law does not have your back on this.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="wrongbox">
<b>Things TikTok told you that are wrong</b>
<ul>
<li>"Wisconsin is a constitutional carry state so they cannot disarm me during a stop." Wrong. <em>Terry v. Ohio</em> allows officers to disarm you during a lawful stop if they have reasonable suspicion you are armed and dangerous. Constitutional carry does not override this.</li>
<li>"If the officer takes longer than 15 minutes, you can leave." There is no magic number. <em>Rodriguez</em> says "reasonably required to complete the mission."</li>
<li>"You have to let them search if they say they smell weed." Not anymore. Marijuana is legal in Wisconsin for medical use. Smell alone is not probable cause. But if they claim to see contraband in plain view, that is different.</li>
<li>"If you do not roll down your window, they cannot ticket you." Wrong. You must display your license. Shoving it through a cracked window satisfies the statute but makes the officer suspicious, which is not illegal but is also not smart.</li>
</ul>
</div>
