# Stand Your Ground / Self-Defense Rights - Rhode Island

## One-Sentence Verdict

Rhode Island is a duty-to-retreat state with a statutory Castle Doctrine: a person must retreat if they can do so safely before using deadly force in public, but there is a rebuttable presumption of reasonable self-defense when using force against a person breaking into a building, and no duty to retreat inside one's own home.

## Verbatim Quotes Anchored to Character Offsets

### R.I. Gen. Laws Sec. 11-8-8 - Castle Doctrine / Rebuttable Presumption

> "In the event that any person shall die or shall sustain a personal injury in any way or for any cause while in the commission of any criminal offense enumerated in Sec. 11-8-2 - 11-8-6, it shall be rebuttably presumed as a matter of law in any civil or criminal proceeding that the owner, tenant, or occupier of the place where the offense was committed acted by reasonable means in self-defense and in the reasonable belief that the person engaged in the criminal offense was about to inflict great bodily harm or death upon that person or any other individual lawfully in the place where the criminal offense was committed. There shall be no duty on the part of an owner, tenant, or occupier to retreat from any person engaged in the commission of any criminal offense enumerated in Sec. 11-8-2 - 11-8-6."

## Castle Doctrine Scope

- **Buildings (Including Dwelling)**: Strong Castle Doctrine under Sec. 11-8-8. The statute creates a rebuttable presumption that the owner/tenant/occupier acted reasonably in self-defense against a person committing breaking and entering. Applies to "dwelling houses" and "any other building, including banks, churches, schools, and even ships and railroad cars."
- **Breaking and Entering Required**: The Castle Doctrine presumption only applies against persons committing breaking and entering offenses defined in Sec. 11-8-2 through 11-8-6.
- **Cohabitant Exception**: State v. Quarles (1986) held that a person assaulted in their own home by a cohabitant must attempt to retreat before using deadly force.
- **Vehicle**: Not explicitly covered by Castle Doctrine statute.
- **Workplace**: Not explicitly covered.

---

## Distinctive State Features

1. **Rebuttable Presumption in Buildings**: Rhode Island's Castle Doctrine is unusually strong in creating a rebuttable presumption of reasonable self-defense in both civil and criminal proceedings - but only in buildings against break-ins.
2. **Cohabitant Retreat Requirement**: State v. Quarles established that the Castle Doctrine does not apply when the attacker is a cohabitant; retreat must be attempted first.
3. **Broad Building Coverage**: The statute applies to virtually any building, not just dwellings, as long as the defender is an occupant and the attacker is breaking in.
4. **No SYG Statute**: Rhode Island has repeatedly rejected SYG legislation. The duty to retreat in public remains the rule.

## Recording Consent Rules

Rhode Island is a **two-party consent** state for audio recordings. R.I. Gen. Laws Sec. 11-35-21 makes it a crime to record a private communication without the consent of all parties.

## Practical Notes

- Rhode Island's Castle Doctrine is strong but narrow. The rebuttable presumption only applies in buildings against persons committing breaking and entering.
- The cohabitant retreat requirement (State v. Quarles) is a critical limitation. Domestic violence situations may not receive full Castle Doctrine protection.
- In public, the duty to retreat is real. Failing to retreat when a safe avenue exists can result in criminal liability.
- Rhode Island has strict gun control laws, including a concealed carry permit requirement and assault weapon restrictions.
- The rebuttable presumption shifts the burden to the prosecution to disprove self-defense, which is a significant advantage for the defender in court.
