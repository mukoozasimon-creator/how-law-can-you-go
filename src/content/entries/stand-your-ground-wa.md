## The Short Answer

Washington is **NOT a formal stand-your-ground state**, but it also does **not have a statutory duty to retreat**. You may use force in self-defense when you reasonably believe it is necessary to prevent imminent unlawful force against yourself or another (RCW 9A.16.020). Washington courts have held that while there is no statutory duty to retreat, the feasibility of retreat may be considered by a jury in assessing whether your belief that force was necessary was reasonable. Deadly force is only justified to prevent imminent death or great bodily harm.

## Key Statutes

### RCW 9A.16.020 - Use of Force - When Lawful

> "The use, attempt, or offer to use force upon or toward the person of another is not unlawful in the following cases: (1) Whenever necessarily used by a public officer... (2) Whenever necessarily used by a person arresting one who has committed a felony... (3) Whenever used by a party about to be injured, or by another lawfully aiding him or her, in preventing or attempting to prevent an offense against his or her person..."

**Live link:** https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=9A.16.020
**Repeal check:** Active. No repeal pending as of August 2026.
**Verdict:** Force justified when necessary to prevent offense against person. No explicit stand-your-ground or duty-to-retreat statute.

### RCW 9A.16.050 - Homicide - By Other Person - When Justifiable

> "Homicide is also justifiable when committed either: (1) In the lawful defense of the slayer, or his or her husband, wife, parent, child, brother, or sister, or of any other person in his or her presence or company, when there is reasonable ground to apprehend a design on the part of the person slain to commit a felony or to do some great personal injury to the slayer or to any such person, and there is imminent danger of such design being accomplished..."

**Live link:** https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=9A.16.050
**Repeal check:** Active. No repeal pending as of August 2026.
**Verdict:** Deadly force justified against imminent felony or great personal injury.

### Washington Case Law - No Statutory Duty to Retreat

> Washington courts have consistently held that there is no statutory duty to retreat before using force in self-defense. However, the availability of retreat may be considered by the jury as one factor in determining whether the defendant's belief that force was necessary was reasonable.

**Verdict:** No statutory duty to retreat, but retreat feasibility may affect reasonableness assessment.

## What This Means for You

| Situation | Duty to Retreat? | Deadly Force Allowed? |
|-----------|---------------|---------------------|
| In your home | No statutory duty | Yes, if imminent death/great bodily harm |
| In public | No statutory duty | Yes, if imminent deadly threat |
| Defending another | No statutory duty | Yes, same standard |
| Jury consideration | Retreat may be considered | As factor in reasonableness |

## Important Notes

- **No formal stand-your-ground statute:** Washington does not have a statute explicitly removing the duty to retreat, nor does it have a statute imposing one.
- **No statutory duty to retreat:** Washington courts have held there is no statutory duty to retreat, but the feasibility of retreat may be considered by the jury.
- **Castle Doctrine by case law:** While not codified in statute, Washington courts recognize enhanced protections for self-defense inside the home.
- **Reasonable belief standard:** Both your belief in the threat and your belief that force is necessary must be objectively reasonable.
- **Initial aggressor exception:** You cannot claim self-defense if you were the initial aggressor, unless you withdrew and communicated withdrawal.
- **Recording:** Washington is a two-party consent state for recordings (RCW 9.73.030). All parties must consent to recording.

## Cross-State Notes

- **vs. Oregon:** OR imposes a duty to retreat before deadly force in public; WA does not statutorily.
- **vs. Idaho:** ID has no formal SYG statute but no duty to retreat by case law; similar to WA.
- **vs. California:** CA has no formal SYG statute but case law confirms no duty to retreat; similar to WA.
