## Summary

New Jersey is one of the most homeschool-friendly states in the U.S. The state requires no notification, no registration, no curriculum approval, no standardized testing, and no parent qualifications. The sole legal requirement is that children ages 6-16 receive "equivalent instruction elsewhere than at school" under N.J.S.A. 18A:38-25. Courts have interpreted "equivalent" broadly - it means comparable, not identical to public school education.

## Legal Framework

### Primary Statute

**N.J.S.A. 18A:38-25** - Compulsory Education Law

> "every parent, guardian or other person having custody and control of a child between six and 16 to ensure that such child regularly attends the public schools of the district or a day school in which there is given instruction equivalent to that provided in the public schools for children of similar grades and attainments or to receive equivalent instruction elsewhere than at school."

- **Instrument**: N.J.S.A. 18A:38-25
- **Live Link**: https://lis.njleg.state.nj.us/nxt/gateway.dll?f=templates&fn=default.htm
- **Repeal Check**: Active as of 2026; no repeal legislation pending
- **Verdict**: The provision "to receive equivalent instruction elsewhere than at school" permits homeschooling without additional state oversight.

### Key Case Law

**State v. Massa**, 95 N.J. Super. 382 (1967)
- Held that "equivalent" does **not** mean "identical" to public school education
- Parents are free to choose their own curriculum, materials, and methods

**State v. Vaughn**, 44 N.J. 142 (1965)
- Established that if parents are charged with truancy, they need only introduce evidence of "equivalent instruction"
- Once evidence is presented, the **burden shifts to the state** to prove the instruction is not equivalent

## Notification

No notification is legally required to homeschool in New Jersey. However, the NJ DOE notes two circumstances where notification is required:

1. If a parent attempts to register a student and the district refuses enrollment, the parent must provide a statement of verification regarding whether the student will attend school elsewhere or receive equivalent instruction elsewhere than at school (N.J.A.C. 6A:22-4.2(d))
2. If withdrawing a student in grades 9-12 from high school, a transfer form must be completed

**Practical Note**: Many families voluntarily send a letter of intent to prevent truancy concerns and create documentation per *State v. Vaughn*.

## Required Subjects

No subjects are mandated. The standard is simply "equivalent instruction." The state cannot review or approve curriculum choices.

## Assessment & Testing

No standardized testing or assessments are required. Homeschoolers cannot be required to take any state or district standardized tests.

## Record-Keeping

No records are required by law. However, keeping basic documentation is recommended in case questions arise.

## Special Provisions

- **High School Transfer**: Grades 9-12 withdrawal requires a transfer form
- **Sports/Activities**: No statutory right to public school extracurricular participation
- **Funding**: No state-level financial programs for homeschoolers

## Distinctive Features

- One of the easiest states to homeschool in - zero regulation beyond the compulsory education statute
- Strong judicial protections (*State v. Vaughn* burden-shifting; *State v. Massa* "equivalent ≠ identical")
- Districts have no authority to approve or deny homeschooling
- No home visits, no portfolio reviews, no curriculum approval
