## The Short Answer

Oregon does **not have a formal stand-your-ground statute**, but the **Oregon Supreme Court held in State v. Sandoval (2007)** that there is **no duty to retreat** before using deadly force in self-defense when faced with an imminent threat of deadly physical force (ORS 161.219). Oregon's self-defense law allows the use of deadly force only against specific threats: (1) a felony involving the use or threatened imminent use of physical force against a person, (2) burglary in a dwelling, or (3) the imminent use of unlawful deadly physical force. The Castle Doctrine is recognized under Oregon law but is not as expansive as in some other states.

## Key Statutes

### ORS 161.209 - Use of Physical Force in Defense of a Person

> "Except as provided in ORS 161.215 and 161.219, a person is justified in using physical force upon another person for self-defense or to defend a third person from what the person reasonably believes to be the use or imminent use of unlawful physical force, and the person may use a degree of force which the person reasonably believes to be necessary for the purpose."

**Live link:** https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors161.html
**Repeal check:** Active. No repeal pending as of August 2026.
**Verdict:** Force justified when reasonably believed necessary against imminent unlawful force.

### ORS 161.219 - Limitations on Use of Deadly Physical Force in Defense of a Person

> "Notwithstanding the provisions of ORS 161.209, a person is not justified in using deadly physical force upon another person unless the person reasonably believes that the other person is: (1) Committing or attempting to commit a felony involving the use or threatened imminent use of physical force against a person; or (2) Committing or attempting to commit a burglary in a dwelling; or (3) Using or about to use unlawful deadly physical force against a person."

**Live link:** https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors161.html
**Repeal check:** Active. No repeal pending as of August 2026.
**Verdict:** Deadly force only justified against three enumerated circumstances.

### State v. Sandoval, 342 Or. 506 (2007) - No Duty to Retreat

> "The legislature did not intend to require a person to retreat before using deadly force against the imminent use of deadly physical force by another. On a purely textual level, ORS 161.219 contains no specific reference to 'retreat', 'escape,' or 'other means of avoiding' a deadly confrontation. Neither, in our view, does it contain any other wording that would suggest a duty of that kind."

**Live link:** https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/
**Repeal check:** Active. Controlling case law as of August 2026.
**Verdict:** Oregon Supreme Court explicitly rejected any duty to retreat before using deadly force against imminent deadly threat.

## What This Means for You

| Situation | Duty to Retreat? | Deadly Force Allowed? |
|-----------|---------------|---------------------|
| In your dwelling | No | Yes, against burglary or imminent deadly force |
| In public | No (Sandoval) | Yes, only against enumerated threats |
| Defending another | No (Sandoval) | Same standard |

## Important Notes

- **No formal SYG statute:** Oregon does not have a statute explicitly removing the duty to retreat. The principle comes from the Oregon Supreme Court's interpretation of ORS 161.219.
- **Limited deadly force justifications:** Oregon restricts deadly force to three specific circumstances: (1) felonies involving force against a person, (2) burglary in a dwelling, and (3) imminent unlawful deadly force. This is more restrictive than many states.
- **No Castle Doctrine statute:** While Oregon recognizes the principle that one need not retreat from their home, there is no explicit Castle Doctrine statute creating presumptions.
- **Burglary in a dwelling:** Oregon allows deadly force against burglary in a dwelling even if the dwelling is not occupied at the time, which is broader than some states.
- **Initial aggressor exception:** You cannot claim self-defense if you were the initial aggressor, unless you withdrew and communicated withdrawal.
- **Recording:** Oregon is a one-party consent state for recordings (ORS 165.540). You may record conversations you are a party to without the other person's consent.

## Cross-State Notes

- **vs. Washington:** Both Pacific Northwest states have no formal SYG statute but no duty to retreat by case law.
- **vs. California:** CA has no formal SYG statute but case law confirms no duty to retreat; similar to OR.
- **vs. Idaho:** ID has no formal SYG statute; similar to OR.
