<div class="quip">Michigan is a two-party consent state on paper, but the Michigan State Police literally put out a memo saying you can record them. So the statute exists, but the cops already surrendered on this one.</div>

<div class="settled">
<b>The stuff that is actually true in Michigan</b>
<ul>
<li><strong>You must show your license, registration, and proof of insurance.</strong> MCL 257.311, 257.225, and 257.328. All three. The officer can demand them on the spot. Failure to display license is a misdemeanor: up to 93 days in jail and $500 fine. But if you produce valid license in court, charge may be reduced.</li>
<li><strong>You can record the officer.</strong> Michigan is technically a two-party consent state (MCL 750.539c), but the Michigan State Police explicitly states that citizens may record police officers performing their duties. Courts have consistently held that recording police in public is protected by the First Amendment. Just do not interfere.</li>
<li><strong>They cannot make you wait for a drug dog.</strong> <em>Rodriguez v. United States</em> (2015). Once the ticket is written, the stop is over. Michigan courts follow this.</li>
<li><strong>You can refuse to consent to a search.</strong> Fourth Amendment. Say "I do not consent to a search." If they have probable cause (smell, plain view), they can search anyway. But your refusal matters in court.</li>
<li><strong>Passengers do not have to show ID.</strong> Michigan has no stop-and-identify statute for mere passengers. But if the officer has reasonable suspicion that the passenger committed a crime, that changes.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="varies">
<b>The grey area where people get themselves arrested</b>
<ul>
<li><strong>"I forgot my license at home."</strong> MCL 257.311 is a misdemeanor. You can be arrested and booked. But if you produce a valid license in court, the charge may be reduced or dismissed.</li>
<li><strong>"I do not answer questions."</strong> True for most questions, but 257.311 requires you to display your license. You cannot refuse that one.</li>
<li><strong>"You cannot search my car."</strong> Correct, but Michigan has a broad plain-view doctrine. If the officer sees contraband, they do not need consent. Also, the smell of marijuana alone is no longer probable cause in Michigan after legalization, but the officer may claim other factors.</li>
<li><strong>"I am driving to a safer location."</strong> No Michigan statute gives you this right. The officer may interpret it as evading. Use your judgment, but know the law does not have your back on this.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="wrongbox">
<b>Things TikTok told you that are wrong</b>
<ul>
<li>"Michigan is a two-party consent state so you cannot record the police." Wrong. The Michigan State Police explicitly says you can. Courts have upheld this under the First Amendment.</li>
<li>"If the officer takes longer than 15 minutes, you can leave." There is no magic number. <em>Rodriguez</em> says "reasonably required to complete the mission."</li>
<li>"You have to let them search if they say they smell weed." Not anymore. Marijuana is legal in Michigan. Smell alone is not probable cause. But if they claim to see contraband in plain view, that is different.</li>
<li>"If you do not roll down your window, they cannot ticket you." Wrong. You must display your license. Shoving it through a cracked window satisfies the statute but makes the officer suspicious, which is not illegal but is also not smart.</li>
</ul>
</div>
