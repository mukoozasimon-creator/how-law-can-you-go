<div class="quip">Every safety guide in America gives you the same advice. In one state it is a felony.</div>

<div class="settled">
<b>The finding that makes this entry worth existing</b>
<p style="margin:0">No state statute we could find says <em>where</em> to pull over. They say stop <strong>promptly</strong>. Meanwhile practically every driver handbook and sheriff's office says "if unsure, slow down, hazards on, continue to a lit public place." That advice has no statutory basis, and whether it is safe depends entirely on how your state drafted its eluding statute.</p>
</div>

<div class="varies">
<b>Same advice, three completely different outcomes</b>
<ul>
<li><strong>Washington: probably no offence.</strong> RCW 46.61.024(1) requires that you fail to stop <em>and</em> drive "in a reckless manner." Reckless driving is an element. Continuing at reduced speed with hazards on is unlikely to satisfy it.</li>
<li><strong>Texas: Class B misdemeanour.</strong> Tex. Transp. Code § 545.421. Bare wilful failure to stop is the offence. But note subsection (b): the officer must be in uniform with badge displayed <em>and</em> the vehicle must bear law-enforcement insignia. An unmarked plain-clothes stop cannot support this charge.</li>
<li><strong>Florida: third-degree felony, from the first second.</strong> Fla. Stat. § 316.1935(1). No recklessness element. The safety advice is a felony exposure that the safety advice does not mention.</li>
<li><strong>Nevada: misdemeanour,</strong> NRS 484B.550, and note the conjunctive: the signal must be by flashing red lamp <strong>and</strong> siren. Lights alone do not satisfy the statute.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="settled">
<b>The two states that actually wrote you a defence</b>
<ul>
<li><strong>Washington, RCW 46.61.024(2).</strong> Affirmative defence if "a reasonable person would not believe that the signal to stop was given by a police officer" <em>and</em> driving on "was reasonable under the circumstances." Best-drafted provision in the country on this problem.</li>
<li><strong>Virginia, Va. Code § 46.2-817.</strong> Affirmative defence where the defendant "reasonably believed he was being pursued by a person other than a law-enforcement officer." Applies to both the misdemeanour and the felony.</li>
<li><strong>Ohio, the sleeper.</strong> R.C. 4549.13 requires traffic-enforcement vehicles to be distinctively marked with a roof light. R.C. 4549.14 then makes an officer who arrests from an unmarked car <strong>incompetent to testify</strong> in a misdemeanour traffic prosecution. That is a hard evidentiary bar, not judicial discretion.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="wrongbox">
<b>What popular advice gets wrong</b>
<ul>
<li>"There's a law that lets you drive to a lit area." We found no such law. Washington and Virginia give you a <strong>defence at trial</strong>, which is not a right at the roadside.</li>
<li>"Call 911 and they'll confirm the stop." We could not verify a single state statute creating that right or immunising you for it, and some agencies say dispatch often cannot confirm in real time.</li>
<li>"Unmarked cars can't pull you over." False generally, but the consequence lands on the <em>charge</em>, not your obligation in the moment. See Ohio and Texas above.</li>
<li>"It's just a ticket if you don't stop right away." Third-degree felony in Florida.</li>
<li>"Hazards on means you're complying." No statute we read makes hazard lights a defence.</li>
</ul>
</div>
