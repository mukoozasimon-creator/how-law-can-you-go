## The Short Answer

Wisconsin does **not have a formal stand-your-ground statute**, but it also does **not have a statutory duty to retreat**. Wisconsin Statute 939.48 allows you to use force in self-defense when you reasonably believe it is necessary to prevent unlawful interference with your person. Wisconsin's **Castle Doctrine** (939.48(1m)) creates a presumption that you reasonably believed deadly force was necessary when someone unlawfully and forcibly enters your dwelling, vehicle, or place of business. However, Wisconsin courts have held that while there is no written duty to retreat, the feasibility of retreat may still be considered by a jury in assessing reasonableness.

## Key Statutes

### Wis. Stat. 939.48(1) - Self-Defense

> "A person is privileged to threaten or intentionally use force against another for the purpose of preventing or terminating what the person reasonably believes to be an unlawful interference with his or her person by such other person. The actor may intentionally use only such force or threat thereof as the actor reasonably believes is necessary to prevent or terminate the interference. The actor may not intentionally use force which is intended or likely to cause death or great bodily harm unless the actor reasonably believes that such force is necessary to prevent imminent death or great bodily harm to himself or herself."

**Live link:** https://docs.legis.wisconsin.gov/document/statutes/939.48
**Repeal check:** Active. No repeal pending as of August 2026.
**Verdict:** Force justified when reasonably believed necessary against unlawful interference. Deadly force only against imminent death or great bodily harm.

### Wis. Stat. 939.48(1m) - Castle Doctrine

> "If an actor intentionally used force that was intended or likely to cause death or great bodily harm, the court may not consider whether the actor had an opportunity to flee or retreat before he or she used force and shall presume that the actor reasonably believed that the force was necessary to prevent imminent death or great bodily harm to himself or herself if the actor makes such a claim under sub. (1) and either of the following applies: (1) The person against whom the force was used was in the process of unlawfully and forcibly entering the actor's dwelling, motor vehicle, or place of business; the actor was present in the dwelling, motor vehicle, or place of business; and the actor knew or reasonably believed that the person had unlawfully and forcibly entered the dwelling, motor vehicle, or place of business. (2) The person against whom the force was used was already in the actor's dwelling, motor vehicle, or place of business after unlawfully and forcibly entering it; the actor was present in the dwelling, motor vehicle, or place of business; and the actor knew or reasonably believed that the person had unlawfully and forcibly entered the dwelling, motor vehicle, or place of business."

**Live link:** https://docs.legis.wisconsin.gov/document/statutes/939.48
**Repeal check:** Active. No repeal pending as of August 2026.
**Verdict:** Presumption of reasonable belief when intruder unlawfully and forcibly enters dwelling, vehicle, or place of business. Court may not consider whether actor had opportunity to retreat.

### Wis. Stat. 939.48(2) - Provocation

> "(a) A person who engages in unlawful conduct of a type likely to provoke others to attack him or her and thereby does provoke an attack is not entitled to claim the privilege of self-defense against such attack, except when the attack which ensues is of a type causing the person engaging in the unlawful conduct to reasonably believe that he or she is in imminent danger of death or great bodily harm. (b) The privilege lost by provocation may be regained if the actor in good faith withdraws from the fight and gives adequate notice thereof to his or her assailant. (c) A person who provokes an attack, whether by lawful or unlawful conduct, with intent to use such an attack as an excuse to cause death or great bodily harm to his or her assailant is not entitled to claim the privilege of self-defense."

**Live link:** https://docs.legis.wisconsin.gov/document/statutes/939.48
**Repeal check:** Active. No repeal pending as of August 2026.
**Verdict:** Initial aggressor cannot claim self-defense unless attack escalates to deadly threat. Privilege regained upon withdrawal.

## What This Means for You

| Situation | Duty to Retreat? | Deadly Force Allowed? |
|-----------|---------------|---------------------|
| In your dwelling (intruder) | No (court may not consider retreat) | Yes, presumed reasonable fear |
| In your vehicle | No (court may not consider retreat) | Yes, presumed reasonable fear |
| In your place of business | No (court may not consider retreat) | Yes, presumed reasonable fear |
| In public | No statutory duty | Yes, if imminent deadly threat |
| Jury consideration | Retreat may affect reasonableness | As factor in reasonableness |

## Important Notes

- **No formal stand-your-ground statute:** Wisconsin does not have a statute explicitly removing the duty to retreat in public. However, there is also no statutory duty to retreat.
- **Castle Doctrine limits retreat consideration:** Under 939.48(1m), the court may not consider whether the actor had an opportunity to flee or retreat before using force in dwelling/vehicle/business cases.
- **Jury may still consider retreat in public:** Wisconsin courts have held that while there is no written duty to retreat, the feasibility of retreat may be considered by the jury as one factor in determining whether the defendant's belief that force was necessary was reasonable.
- **Castle Doctrine applies to business:** Wisconsin extends Castle Doctrine protections to your place of business, not just your home and vehicle.
- **Civil immunity:** Wisconsin provides civil immunity for justified use of force under Wis. Stat. 895.62.
- **Recording:** Wisconsin is a one-party consent state for recordings (Wis. Stat. 968.31). You may record conversations you are a party to without the other person's consent.

## Cross-State Notes

- **vs. Illinois:** Both lack formal SYG statutes but have no statutory duty to retreat. WI has explicit Castle Doctrine; IL relies on case law.
- **vs. Minnesota:** MN imposes a duty to retreat before deadly force in public; WI does not statutorily.
- **vs. Michigan:** MI is a stand-your-ground state with broader protections; WI is more limited.
