<div class="quip">Texas is a one-party consent state, which means you can record the officer. But remember: everything is bigger in Texas, including the officer's patience for your constitutional lectures.</div>

<div class="settled">
<b>The stuff that is actually true in Texas</b>
<ul>
<li><strong>You must show your driver's license on demand.</strong> Tex. Transp. Code 521.025. It is a misdemeanor if you refuse. Up to $200 fine for first offense. Up to 6 months in jail for third offense within a year.</li>
<li><strong>You can record the officer.</strong> Texas is a one-party consent state for audio recordings. You are legally permitted to record your own interactions with police. Just do not interfere - Tex. Penal Code 38.15.</li>
<li><strong>They cannot make you wait for a drug dog.</strong> <em>Rodriguez v. United States</em> (2015). Once the ticket is written, the stop's mission is complete. If they ask you to wait for a K-9, you may politely ask if you are free to leave.</li>
<li><strong>You can refuse to consent to a search.</strong> Fourth Amendment. Say "I do not consent to a search." If they have probable cause (smell, plain view), they can search anyway. But your refusal matters in court.</li>
<li><strong>Passengers do not have to show ID.</strong> Texas has no stop-and-identify statute for mere passengers. But if the officer has reasonable suspicion of criminal activity, that changes.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="varies">
<b>The grey area where people get themselves arrested</b>
<ul>
<li><strong>"I left my license at home."</strong> Tex. Transp. Code 521.025(d) gives you a defense if you produce a valid license in court. But you can still be arrested and booked in the meantime.</li>
<li><strong>"I do not answer questions."</strong> True for most questions, but 521.025 requires you to display your license. You cannot refuse that one.</li>
<li><strong>"You cannot search my car."</strong> Correct, but Texas has broad plain-view and smell doctrines. If the officer claims to smell marijuana, that may support probable cause even though hemp is legal. This is a mess in Texas right now.</li>
<li><strong>"I am driving to a safer location."</strong> No Texas statute gives you this right. Tex. Transp. Code 545.421 makes willful failure to stop a Class B misdemeanor. Be careful.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="wrongbox">
<b>Things TikTok told you that are wrong</b>
<ul>
<li>"Texas is a constitutional carry state so they cannot disarm me during a stop." Wrong. <em>Terry v. Ohio</em> allows officers to disarm you during a lawful stop if they have reasonable suspicion you are armed and dangerous. Constitutional carry does not override this.</li>
<li>"If the officer takes longer than 20 minutes, you can leave." There is no magic number. <em>Rodriguez</em> says "reasonably required to complete the mission."</li>
<li>"You have to let them search if they say they smell weed." Not necessarily. Challenge probable cause in court, not at the roadside.</li>
<li>"Unmarked cars cannot pull you over." False. But if the officer is not in uniform and the vehicle is not marked, that may affect the validity of the stop under Tex. Transp. Code 543.003.</li>
</ul>
</div>
