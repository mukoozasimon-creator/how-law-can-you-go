<div class="quip">New York is a one-party consent state, which means you can record the officer while they write you a ticket for going 36 in a 35. The recording will not get you out of the ticket, but it might get you out of something worse.</div>

<div class="settled">
<b>The stuff that is actually true in New York</b>
<ul>
<li><strong>You must show your license, registration, and proof of insurance.</strong> VTL 509, 401, and 313. All three. The officer can demand them on the spot. It is a traffic infraction, not a crime, but refusing can escalate to obstruction or resisting.</li>
<li><strong>You can record the officer.</strong> New York is a one-party consent state (Penal Law 250.00). You are a party to the conversation. Courts have consistently held that recording police in public is protected by the First Amendment. Just do not interfere.</li>
<li><strong>They cannot make you wait for a drug dog.</strong> <em>Rodriguez v. United States</em> (2015). Once the ticket is written, the stop is over. New York courts follow this.</li>
<li><strong>You can refuse to consent to a search.</strong> Fourth Amendment. Say "I do not consent to a search." If they have probable cause (smell, plain view), they can search anyway. But your refusal matters in court.</li>
<li><strong>Passengers do not have to show ID.</strong> New York has no stop-and-identify statute for mere passengers. CPL 140.50 allows detention on reasonable suspicion but does not compel answering questions. Passengers can decline to ID unless there is independent reasonable suspicion of a crime.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="varies">
<b>The grey area where people get themselves arrested</b>
<ul>
<li><strong>"I forgot my license at home."</strong> VTL 509 is a traffic infraction if you produce it in court. But refusing to exhibit it to the officer can lead to arrest for obstruction or resisting. The distinction matters.</li>
<li><strong>"I do not answer questions."</strong> True for most questions, but VTL 509 requires you to exhibit your license. You cannot refuse that one.</li>
<li><strong>"You cannot search my car."</strong> Correct, but New York has a broad plain-view doctrine. If the officer sees contraband, they do not need consent. Also, the smell of marijuana alone is no longer probable cause in New York after legalization, but the officer may claim other factors.</li>
<li><strong>"I am driving to a safer location."</strong> No New York statute gives you this right. The officer may interpret it as evading. Use your judgment, but know the law does not have your back on this.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="wrongbox">
<b>Things TikTok told you that are wrong</b>
<ul>
<li>"New York City is a sanctuary city so they cannot ask for ID." Wrong. VTL 509 applies statewide. Immigration status is irrelevant to a traffic stop.</li>
<li>"If the officer takes longer than 10 minutes, you can leave." There is no magic number. <em>Rodriguez</em> says "reasonably required to complete the mission."</li>
<li>"You have to let them search if they say they smell weed." Not anymore. Marijuana is legal in New York. Smell alone is not probable cause. But if they claim to see contraband in plain view, that is different.</li>
<li>"If you do not roll down your window, they cannot ticket you." Wrong. You must exhibit your license. Shoving it through a cracked window satisfies the statute but makes the officer suspicious, which is not illegal but is also not smart.</li>
</ul>
</div>
