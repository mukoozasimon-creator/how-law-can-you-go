## The Short Answer

North Carolina is a **stand-your-ground state**. You have **no duty to retreat** before using force in self-defense if you are in a place where you have a lawful right to be and reasonably believe you face imminent death or great bodily harm (N.C.G.S. 14-51.3). North Carolina's **Castle Doctrine** (N.C.G.S. 14-51.2) creates statutory presumptions of reasonable fear when someone unlawfully and forcibly enters your home (including curtilage), vehicle, or workplace. The NC Supreme Court has reinforced that these statutes create a presumption-driven framework, not a generic reasonableness inquiry (State v. Allison, 2025; State v. Phillips, 2024).

## Key Statutes

### N.C.G.S. 14-51.2 - Home, Motor Vehicle, and Workplace Protection

> "(b) The lawful occupant of a home, motor vehicle, or workplace is presumed to have held a reasonable fear of imminent death or serious bodily harm to himself or herself or another when using defensive force that is intended or likely to cause death or great bodily harm to another if both of the following apply: (1) The person against whom the defensive force was used was in the process of unlawfully and forcefully entering, or had unlawfully and forcibly entered, a home, motor vehicle, or workplace, or if that person had removed or was attempting to remove another against that person's will from the home, motor vehicle, or workplace. (2) The person who uses defensive force knew or had reason to believe that an unlawful and forcible entry or unlawful and forcible act was occurring or had occurred."

**Live link:** https://www.ncleg.gov/Laws/GeneralStatutes/Chapter14/GS_14-51.2
**Repeal check:** Active. No repeal pending as of August 2026.
**Verdict:** Presumption of reasonable fear for lawful occupant during unlawful and forcible entry into home, vehicle, or workplace.

### N.C.G.S. 14-51.3 - Use of Force in Defense of Person

> "A person is justified in using force, except deadly force, against another when and to the extent that the person reasonably believes that the conduct is necessary to defend himself or herself or another against the other's imminent use of unlawful force. A person is justified in the use of deadly force and does not have a duty to retreat in any place he or she has the lawful right to be if both of the following apply: (1) The person reasonably believes that such force is necessary to prevent imminent death or great bodily harm to himself or herself or another. (2) The person is not engaged in criminal activity and is not the initial aggressor."

**Live link:** https://www.ncleg.gov/Laws/GeneralStatutes/Chapter14/GS_14-51.3
**Repeal check:** Active. No repeal pending as of August 2026.
**Verdict:** No duty to retreat anywhere you have a lawful right to be. Deadly force justified against imminent death or great bodily harm.

### N.C.G.S. 14-51.2(c) - Rebuttal of Presumption

> "The presumption set forth in subsection (b) of this section shall be rebuttable and does not apply in any of the following circumstances: (1) The person against whom the defensive force is used has the right to be in the home, motor vehicle, or workplace... (2) The person sought to be removed is a child or grandchild... (3) The person using defensive force is engaged in criminal activity... (4) The person against whom the defensive force is used is a law enforcement officer... (5) The person using force knew or reasonably should have known that the person against whom the defensive force was used was a lawful resident..."

**Live link:** https://www.ncleg.gov/Laws/GeneralStatutes/Chapter14/GS_14-51.2
**Repeal check:** Active. No repeal pending as of August 2026.
**Verdict:** Five statutory circumstances that rebut the Castle Doctrine presumption.

### N.C.G.S. 14-51.4 - Justification for Use of Force Not Available

> "The justification described in G.S. 14-51.2 and G.S. 14-51.3 is not available to a person who used force and who was engaged in criminal conduct or who was the initial aggressor..."

**Live link:** https://www.ncleg.gov/Laws/GeneralStatutes/Chapter14/GS_14-51.4
**Repeal check:** Active. No repeal pending as of August 2026.
**Verdict:** Self-defense not available if engaged in criminal conduct or as initial aggressor.

## What This Means for You

| Situation | Duty to Retreat? | Deadly Force Allowed? |
|-----------|---------------|---------------------|
| In your home (including curtilage) | No | Yes, presumed reasonable fear |
| In your occupied vehicle | No | Yes, presumed reasonable fear |
| In your workplace | No | Yes, presumed reasonable fear |
| In public (lawfully present) | No | Yes, if imminent deadly threat |
| Defending another | No | Yes, same standard |

## Important Notes

- **No duty to retreat:** N.C.G.S. 14-51.3 explicitly removes the duty to retreat anywhere you have a lawful right to be.
- **Castle Doctrine includes curtilage:** The NC Supreme Court in State v. Allison (2025) confirmed that "home" under 14-51.2 includes the curtilage (porch, doorway, yard). The trial court erred by not instructing the jury on this.
- **Presumption-driven framework:** The NC Supreme Court in State v. Phillips (2024) and State v. Allison (2025) reinforced that the Castle Doctrine creates a statutory presumption framework. Once trigger facts are found, the State must rebut through the five circumstances in 14-51.2(c). Jurors cannot disregard the presumption through a free-form reasonableness evaluation.
- **Civil and criminal immunity:** Justified use of force provides immunity from both criminal prosecution and civil liability.
- **Initial aggressor exception:** You cannot claim self-defense if you were the initial aggressor or engaged in criminal activity.
- **Recording:** North Carolina is a one-party consent state for recordings (N.C.G.S. 15A-287). You may record conversations you are a party to without the other person's consent.

## Cross-State Notes

- **vs. South Carolina:** Both are stand-your-ground states with Castle Doctrine protections.
- **vs. Georgia:** Both are stand-your-ground states. NC's Castle Doctrine has a more structured presumption framework.
- **vs. Virginia:** VA imposes a duty to retreat in public; NC does not.
