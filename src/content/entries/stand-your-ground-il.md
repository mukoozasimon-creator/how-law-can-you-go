## The Short Answer

Illinois does **not have a formal stand-your-ground statute**, but the Illinois Supreme Court has held through case law that there is **no duty to retreat** before using force in public when faced with a threat of death or great bodily harm. Illinois's **Castle Doctrine** (720 ILCS 5/7-2) explicitly removes the duty to retreat inside your dwelling and creates a rebuttable presumption that an intruder entering violently poses a lethal threat. You may use deadly force only if you reasonably believe it is necessary to prevent imminent death or great bodily harm, or to prevent the commission of a forcible felony.

## Key Statutes

### 720 ILCS 5/7-1 - Use of Force in Defense of Person

> "A person is justified in the use of force against another when and to the extent that he reasonably believes that such conduct is necessary to defend himself or another against such other's imminent use of unlawful force."

### 720 ILCS 5/7-2 - Use of Force in Defense of Dwelling

> "(a) A person is justified in the use of force against another when and to the extent that he reasonably believes that such conduct is necessary to prevent or terminate such other's unlawful entry into or attack upon a dwelling. (b) However, he is justified in the use of force which is intended or likely to cause death or great bodily harm only if: (1) The entry is made or attempted in a violent, riotous, or tumultuous manner, and he reasonably believes that such force is necessary to prevent an assault upon, or offer of personal violence to, him or another then in the dwelling..."

### 720 ILCS 5/7-3 - Use of Force in Defense of Other Property

> "A person is justified in the use of force against another when and to the extent that he reasonably believes that such conduct is necessary to prevent or terminate the trespass or conversion of property..."

### People v. Aguilar (2013) - No Duty to Retreat in Public

> The Illinois Supreme Court held that the Second Amendment protects the right to carry firearms outside the home for self-defense, and that there is no duty to retreat before using force in self-defense when faced with an imminent threat of death or great bodily harm.

## What This Means for You

| Situation | Duty to Retreat? | Deadly Force Allowed? |
|-----------|---------------|---------------------|
| In your dwelling (violent intruder) | No | Yes, if reasonable belief of imminent assault |
| In public | No (case law) | Yes, if imminent threat of death/great bodily harm |
| Defending another | No (case law) | Yes, same standard |
| Defending property only | No | No - deadly force not justified solely for property |

## Important Notes

- **No formal stand-your-ground statute:** Illinois does not have a statute explicitly removing the duty to retreat in public. However, the Illinois Supreme Court has held through case law (People v. Aguilar, 2013) that there is no duty to retreat.
- **Castle Doctrine limited to dwelling:** The statutory Castle Doctrine (720 ILCS 5/7-2) applies only to your dwelling. Deadly force is justified only against violent/riotous/tumultuous entry.
- **Forcible felony exception:** Deadly force is also justified to prevent the commission of a forcible felony (e.g., burglary of an occupied vehicle under 720 ILCS 5/19-1).
- **Initial aggressor exception:** You cannot claim self-defense if you were the initial aggressor, unless you withdrew and communicated withdrawal.
- **Proportionality:** Force must be proportional to the threat.
- **Recording:** Illinois is a two-party consent state for recordings (720 ILCS 5/14-2). All parties must consent to recording.

## Cross-State Notes

- **vs. Indiana:** IN has an explicit stand-your-ground statute; IL relies on case law.
- **vs. Wisconsin:** WI has an explicit stand-your-ground statute (2011); IL does not.
- **vs. Ohio:** OH enacted explicit stand-your-ground legislation in 2021; IL relies on judicial precedent.
