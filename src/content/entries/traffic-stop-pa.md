<div class="quip">Pennsylvania is a two-party consent state, which means you can record the officer. But if you are from Philly, you already knew that because you have been recording everything since 2008.</div>

<div class="settled">
<b>The stuff that is actually true in Pennsylvania</b>
<ul>
<li><strong>You must show your license, registration, and proof of insurance.</strong> 75 Pa.C.S. 1501, 1301, and 1786. All three. Summary offenses. The officer can demand them on the spot.</li>
<li><strong>You can record the officer.</strong> Pennsylvania is a two-party consent state (18 Pa.C.S. 5703), but police performing public duties have no reasonable expectation of privacy. Courts have consistently held that recording police in public is protected by the First Amendment. Just do not interfere.</li>
<li><strong>They cannot make you wait for a drug dog.</strong> <em>Rodriguez v. United States</em> (2015). Once the ticket is written, the stop is over. Pennsylvania courts follow this.</li>
<li><strong>You can refuse to consent to a search.</strong> Fourth Amendment. Say "I do not consent to a search." If they have probable cause (smell, plain view), they can search anyway. But your refusal matters in court.</li>
<li><strong>Passengers do not have to show ID.</strong> Pennsylvania has no stop-and-identify statute for mere passengers. But if the officer has reasonable suspicion that the passenger committed a crime, that changes.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="varies">
<b>The grey area where people get themselves arrested</b>
<ul>
<li><strong>"I forgot my license at home."</strong> 75 Pa.C.S. 1501 is a summary offense. You can be fined and your license may be suspended. But it is not a crime. Refusing to exhibit it to the officer, however, can lead to arrest for obstruction.</li>
<li><strong>"I do not answer questions."</strong> True for most questions, but 1501 requires you to exhibit your license. You cannot refuse that one.</li>
<li><strong>"You cannot search my car."</strong> Correct, but Pennsylvania has a broad plain-view doctrine. If the officer sees contraband, they do not need consent. Also, the smell of marijuana alone is no longer probable cause in Pennsylvania after legalization, but the officer may claim other factors.</li>
<li><strong>"I am driving to a safer location."</strong> No Pennsylvania statute gives you this right. The officer may interpret it as evading. Use your judgment, but know the law does not have your back on this.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="wrongbox">
<b>Things TikTok told you that are wrong</b>
<ul>
<li>"Pennsylvania is a constitutional carry state so they cannot disarm me during a stop." Wrong. <em>Terry v. Ohio</em> allows officers to disarm you during a lawful stop if they have reasonable suspicion you are armed and dangerous. Constitutional carry does not override this.</li>
<li>"If the officer takes longer than 15 minutes, you can leave." There is no magic number. <em>Rodriguez</em> says "reasonably required to complete the mission."</li>
<li>"You have to let them search if they say they smell weed." Not anymore. Marijuana is legal in Pennsylvania. Smell alone is not probable cause. But if they claim to see contraband in plain view, that is different.</li>
<li>"If you do not roll down your window, they cannot ticket you." Wrong. You must exhibit your license. Shoving it through a cracked window satisfies the statute but makes the officer suspicious, which is not illegal but is also not smart.</li>
</ul>
</div>
