<div class="quip">California will let you record the cop, but you better have your license ready because they are absolutely going to ask for it.</div>

<div class="settled">
<b>The stuff that is actually true in California</b>
<ul>
<li><strong>You must show your license, registration, and proof of insurance.</strong> CVC 12951, 4462, and 16028. All three. No exceptions. The officer does not need probable cause to ask - it is a lawful demand incident to the stop.</li>
<li><strong>You can record the officer.</strong> California is a two-party consent state (Penal Code 632), but police performing public duties have no reasonable expectation of privacy. Courts have upheld this repeatedly. Just do not interfere.</li>
<li><strong>They cannot make you wait for a drug dog.</strong> <em>Rodriguez v. United States</em> (2015). Once the ticket is written, the stop is over. If they ask you to stick around while a K-9 unit drives over, you are free to leave - but say it politely.</li>
<li><strong>You can refuse to consent to a search.</strong> Fourth Amendment. Say "I do not consent to a search." They may still search if they have probable cause (smell of weed, plain view contraband), but your refusal preserves your rights for court.</li>
<li><strong>Passengers do not have to show ID.</strong> California has no stop-and-identify statute for mere passengers. But if the officer has reasonable suspicion that the passenger committed a crime, that changes.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="varies">
<b>The grey area where people get themselves arrested</b>
<ul>
<li><strong>"I forgot my license at home."</strong> CVC 12951(a) makes this a fix-it ticket if you produce it in court. But 12951(b) makes refusing to present it a misdemeanor. The distinction is whether you forgot it or refused to show it.</li>
<li><strong>"I do not answer questions."</strong> True for most questions, but CVC 12951 specifically requires you to present your license. You cannot silence-your-way out of that one.</li>
<li><strong>"You cannot search my car."</strong> Correct, but if they smell weed or see something illegal in plain view, they do not need your consent. California decriminalized recreational cannabis, but driving under the influence is still DUI and open container in a vehicle is still CVC 23222.</li>
<li><strong>"I am driving to a safer location."</strong> There is no California statute giving you this right. The officer may interpret it as evading. Use your judgment, but know the law does not have your back on this.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="wrongbox">
<b>Things TikTok told you that are wrong</b>
<ul>
<li>"If you do not roll down your window all the way, they cannot ticket you." Wrong. You must present your documents. Rolling the window down two inches and shoving your license through the crack satisfies the statute, but it also makes the officer suspicious, which is not a crime but is also not a vibe.</li>
<li>"California is a sanctuary state so they cannot ask for ID." Wrong. CVC 12951 applies to everyone driving a motor vehicle. Immigration status is irrelevant to a traffic stop.</li>
<li>"If the officer takes longer than 15 minutes, you can leave." There is no magic number. <em>Rodriguez</em> says "reasonably required to complete the mission." That varies by stop.</li>
<li>"You have to let them search if they say they smell weed." Not necessarily. Smell can support probable cause, but it is not automatic. Challenge it in court, not at the roadside.</li>
</ul>
</div>
